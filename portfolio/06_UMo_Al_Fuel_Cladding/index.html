UMo-Al Monolithic Fuel and Cladding-Interface Modeling

Atomistic modeling of U-10Mo fuel, Zr diffusion barriers, AA6061 cladding, HIP-bonded interfaces, diffusion, residual-stress mechanisms, and delamination risk in monolithic research-reactor fuel plates.

← Back to Research Portfolio
Engineering objective: determine how fuel composition, Zr diffusion barriers, AA6061 chemistry, HIP processing, interface structure, diffusion, defects, and mechanical loading influence fuel-plate integrity and the initiation of fuel-cladding or cladding-cladding separation.

Fuel-Plate Architecture

The physical system of interest is a layered monolithic fuel plate built around AA6061 cladding | Zr diffusion barrier | U-10Mo fuel | Zr diffusion barrier | AA6061 cladding. The Zr layers restrict chemical interaction between the U-Mo fuel and Al-based cladding, while hot isostatic pressing consolidates the assembly. Atomic-scale behavior at these interfaces influences bond strength, diffusion, residual stress, local phase formation, and eventual delamination.

Why This Matters

Fuel Integrity

Monolithic U-Mo fuels are designed for high uranium density while supporting conversion from highly enriched to low-enriched uranium research-reactor fuels. Interface integrity is therefore central to reliable plate performance.

HIP Bonding

HIP creates the bonded plate, but thermal history, oxide state, precipitates, diffusion, and mechanical-property mismatch can influence residual stress and bond strength.

Diffusion Barrier

The Zr interlayer is intended to suppress unfavorable fuel-cladding chemical interaction. Modeling can help identify how its thickness, structure, and interfaces affect transport and bonding.

Delamination Risk

Normal separation, local void formation, interface mixing, irradiation defects, and stress concentration can be studied at atomic scale to identify mechanisms that may precede macroscopic separation.

Simulation Program

1. Constituent validation: establish stable U-Mo, Zr, and AA6061 atomistic models and verify structural, thermal, and mechanical behavior against appropriate references.
2. Interface construction: construct AA6061/AA6061, Zr/AA6061, U-Mo/Zr, and eventually the full AA6061/Zr/U-Mo/Zr/AA6061 stack.
3. HIP-relevant thermal cycle: minimize, heat toward the HIP temperature range, hold, cool, and monitor atomic rearrangement, interface mixing, residual structure, and free-volume evolution.
4. Mechanical separation: apply interface-normal tensile loading to obtain peak traction, separation behavior, work of separation, defect nucleation, and the atomistic pathway to delamination.
5. Irradiation / post-irradiation extension: introduce defects or displacement-cascade conditions after the baseline interface physics is validated.

Quantities Extracted from MD

Interface Mechanics

Traction-separation response, peak interface stress, critical opening displacement, work of separation, local stress concentration, void initiation, and failure morphology.

Diffusion & Mixing

Species-resolved concentration profiles, mixing width, mean-squared displacement, migration across Zr/Al and U-Mo/Zr interfaces, and temperature-dependent transport.

Atomic Structure

Local coordination, structural disorder, phase/chemical ordering indicators, free volume, defect evolution, and interface reconstruction during thermal and mechanical loading.

Engineering Translation

Identify interface states and processing conditions that are more resistant to diffusion-driven degradation or delamination and prioritize experimental HIP, microscopy, and mechanical-validation conditions.

Model-Validation Boundary

Important: geometry visualization and constituent models can be shown as model construction. Quantitative full-stack U-Mo-Zr-Al diffusion, stress, or delamination results should only be reported after selecting and validating a compatible interatomic-potential description with defensible cross interactions across U, Mo, Zr, Al, and relevant AA6061 solutes.
